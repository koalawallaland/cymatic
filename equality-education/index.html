Equality & Education

A collection of original laws, historical footage, survivor testimony, maps and teaching resources on exclusion, resistance and civil rights in America.

Begin with the record, then listen to the people who lived it. Ask whose voices, rights and histories have been excluded.

Content note: some sources describe racial violence, coercion and child abuse. For younger learners, preview materials and use age-appropriate excerpts. Links lead to external archives; availability may vary.

CH. 01 · 1871–1943

Chinese Exclusion & Angel Island

The 1882 law barred most Chinese laborers from immigrating and enforced exclusion through documentation, interrogations and detention. Angel Island, operating as an immigration station from 1910 to 1940, preserves poems carved into the barracks by detainees. Exclusion was repealed in 1943, but restrictive quotas remained.

CH. 02 · 1942–1945; redress

Japanese American incarceration

Executive Order 9066 enabled forced removal and wartime incarceration of roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, many U.S. citizens. Manzanar and Tule Lake were among the California sites. Use survivor testimony alongside official records; government films can reveal propaganda rather than describe lived experiences accurately.

CH. 03 · 19th–20th centuries

Sundown towns & the Green Book

Communities across the United States enforced racial exclusion through threats, violence, housing rules, policing or local customs. Many targeted Black Americans; some targeted other groups as well. The historical Green Book helped Black travelers locate safer accommodations. Historical database entries vary in certainty and are not present-day safety assessments.

CH. 04 · 1964–1969

Public spaces & the right to belong

On June 18, 1964, James Brock poured acid into the Monson Motor Lodge pool during an interracial civil rights swim-in in St. Augustine, Florida. The next day the Senate passed the Civil Rights Act; it became law July 2. In 1969, Fred Rogers invited Officer Clemmons to share his wading pool, modeling racial inclusion on children's television.

CH. 05 · 1946–1954 and beyond

School segregation & civil rights

Mendez v. Westminster challenged segregation of Mexican American children in Southern California. The 1946 ruling and 1947 appeal preceded Brown v. Board of Education (1954), which held state-mandated public-school racial segregation unconstitutional. Legal victories did not instantly eliminate segregation.

CH. 06 · 20th century

Redlining & restrictive covenants

Racial covenants restricted property ownership or occupancy, while discriminatory lending and underwriting practices restricted access to credit. These were distinct mechanisms that could reinforce segregation. Historical HOLC maps document racialized assessments of neighborhood mortgage risk; they are not a complete record of every discriminatory lender or policy.

CH. 07 · 19th–20th centuries

Indigenous boarding schools

Federal and associated religious boarding-school systems forcibly assimilated many Indigenous children by separating them from families, languages and cultures. The Department of the Interior's 2024 investigation expanded its inventory to 417 institutions. Survivor accounts and tribal perspectives are essential.

CH. 08 · 1871 onward

California & Los Angeles field notes

Read local history as part of the national story: anti-Chinese mob violence in Los Angeles in 1871; exclusion at Angel Island; incarceration at Manzanar; school desegregation in Orange County; racial restrictions in housing and town boundaries. Local historical records need corroboration and careful distinction between past and present.
